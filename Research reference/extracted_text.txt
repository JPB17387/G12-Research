SCOPE AND DELIMITATION OF THE STUDY


This study aims to identify and analyze the determinants of smartphone addiction among students in Dumanjug National High School. This scope is limited to the students of Dumanjug National High School and the determinants of smartphone addiction includes: craving, social relationships (frequency and enjoyment of social communications), social relationships(behaviours and consequences), smartphone addiction(physical discomfort), smartphone addiction(consistency in work throughout the term).

This study will be limited to SHS students enrolled in Dumanjug National High School during the school year 2024-2025. It will not include students from other regions or educational levels. The study will focus on the overall impact of smartphone addiction on academic performance and will not delve into the influence of specific smartphone apps. Additionally, the study will not explore other factors that may affect academic performance, such as socio-economic status, learning disabilities, or family support. The study will also not examine the long-term consequences of smartphone addiction or the effectiveness of potential interventions. Furthermore, the study will only include a sample of 242 students, selected through randomization due to time and resource constraints

REVIEW OF RELATED LITERATURE

         Today’s youth are obtaining access to smartphones at younger and younger ages (Bennet,2020). Although there are many studies in the literature for the factors influencing the use of smartphone, the majority of these studies are for general use of mobile phones. The research on addition of mobile phone is limited (Zuhal and Volkan,2016).
         Smartphone addiction, also referred to as problematic smartphone use, is a behavioural addiction characterised by excessive or compulsive use of smartphones. It has become a growing concern, particularly among students, due to it’s potential to interfere with daily activities, including academic performance. This review synthesis key literature on the determinants of smartphone addiction and how it affects academic outcomes.
         Smartphone is an electronic gadget mostly used for daily two-way communication (Nair, Vjay & Vinod,2014). It is a device used as a communication tool (Khan, Khalid & labal,2019). In the past few years, it has become so common that individuals, especially youth cannot even think of their lives without using smartphones (Philips & Bianchi,2005)
          According to Sheila and Steve (2020) due to the high accessibility and mobility of smartphones, widespread and persuasive smartphone use has become the social norm, exposing users to various health and other risk factors. There is however a debate on whether addiction to smartphone is a valid behavioural addiction that is distinct from similar conditions. However according to Gath et al. (2024) smartphones are an unavoidable part of modern life. While some research suggests that they are incongruous with educational success and well-being for children and youth, other research advocates for the beneficial role it can play at school.
         A smartphone is a gadget that can make and receive calls when connected to a network. This gadget does not require a person to stay in one place as they are able to move around within any geographical area that has cell network connectivity. A smartphone gets interconnected to an associated network provided by a mobile phone operator, allowing access to the public telephone network. Today, the smartphone is no longer limited to making and receiving calls. There are value-added services such as text messaging, sending email, online chatting, internet access, photography and gaming. In this borderless global era, mobile communication has become part of our life regardless of region, culture, age and community. Seong and Bo(2018) agreed that smartphone addiction is already entrenched with internet addiction as the symptoms and effects, either positive or negative, are similar. Plant(2001) indicated that there is no standard smartphone effect although the same attitudes, rituals, responses and emotional engagements were discovered. Spending four hours on the smartphone can be considered as addiction, though such criteria of spending seven hours on the smartphone was commonly categorised as a sign of addiction.  
               Smartphone addiction could be considered a form of technological addiction (Derounian,2017). Nevertheless, apart from the person-to-person description, uncontrollable usage of smartphone can effect academic performance. Smartphone addiction can be categorized as a restlessness symptom associated with attempts to frequently check the phone even though there are no notifications (Ling,2024). Besides that, this addiction is defined as a strong desire to use the smartphone in order to remain occupied by surfing the internet, shopping online and playing games. Students interacting with their phones out of habit can be a way to avoid communicating with parents or teachers. Some students may experience withdrawal symptoms such as insomnia, anxiety, and depression when they are without their smartphone. Rice et al.(2018) exposed that smartphone usage relates to occupation, income and marital status of the potential users. Though many current studies have shown smartphone usage benefits in learning, Woodcock et al. (2012) demonstrated contrasting findings. Students were found always using their phones more for playing games and other leisure activities than for learning. Among the identified factors preventing widespread adoption of smartphone for education are physical, technical and psychological limitations. White and Mills(2012) also found that students were increasingly adopting smartphones with the focus on personal use rather  than education. In this study, students were asked if they used smartphone applications in categories in education, dictionary, games and maps. Similarly, Anzai et al.(2013) investigated mobile device use for mobile photo, note-taking, in order to examine the effect of this trend on vocabulary retention.

Determinants of Smartphone Addiction 
          Several psychological, social, and technological factors contribute to the development of smartphone addiction. 

Psychological Determinants
Personality Traits: Research has shown that certain personality traits are linked with higher risks of smartphone addiction. Choliz (2012) and Billieux et al. (2015) found that individuals with impulsive tendencies, low self-control, and emotional instability are more likely to engage in compulsive smartphone use. Additionally,  Elhai et al. (2017) showed that traits like neuroticism are associated with excessive smartphone use as a coping mechanism for emotional difficulties.
Anxiety & Stress: Smartphone use as a stress-relief tool has been explored in multiple studies. ELhai et al. (2017) concluded that students with higher levels of anxiety and stress tend to use smartphones excessively, seeking comfort through social media and entertainment apps. Leung (2008) supported this, showing that stress and smartphone overuse from a vicious cycle, where stress leads to overuse, which in turn bates stress.
Social Anxiety: Hong et al. (2012) demonstrated that socially anxious individuals often turn to smartphone to escape real-life social interactions. The reliance on virtual interactions increases their likelihood of becoming addicted to smartphone, as they avoid in person communication.

RECOMMENDATIONS
     We can recommend several tips to help students avoid smartphone addiction:
     1. Set limits: Establish specific time periods during the day when you use your phone, and stick to those limits.
     2. Create a schedule: Plan your day in advance and allocate time for studying, socializing, exercising, and other activities that don't involve your phone.
     3. Use app blockers: Consider using apps or features that limit your access to certain apps or websites that may be contributing to your smartphone addiction.
     4. Practice mindfulness: Be aware of your phone usage and how it affects your mood, productivity, and relationships. Take breaks and practice mindfulness techniques to stay present and focused.
     5. Find alternative activities: Replace phone usage with other activities you enjoy, such as reading a book, going for a walk, or having a conversation with a friend.
     6. Engage in hobbies: Cultivate hobbies and interests that don't involve your phone, such as art, music, sports, or cooking.
     7. Socialize offline: Make an effort to spend time with friends and family in person, rather than relying solely on digital communication.
     8. Turn off notifications: Disable unnecessary notifications on your phone to reduce the temptation to constantly check it.
     9. Designate phone-free zones: Establish certain areas in your home or specific times of day where phone usage is not allowed, such as during meals or before bedtime.
     10. Practice digital detox: Take regular breaks from your phone, such as setting aside a "tech-free" day once a week to disconnect and focus on other activities.
     11. Use a physical timer: Set a timer when using your phone for a specific purpose or leisure activity to help you stay mindful of your screen time.
     12. Seek support: Talk to friends, family, or a counselor about your struggles with smartphone addiction and ask for their understanding and encouragement as you work towards reducing your phone usage.
     13. Reflect on triggers: Identify the situations or emotions that lead you to use your phone excessively and develop healthy coping strategies to address them, such as going for a walk, practicing deep breathing, or journaling.
     Remember, it's essential to be patient and kind to yourself as you navigate the process of reducing your smartphone usage. Small changes and consistent efforts can make a significant difference in your life.

Appendix A
Research Output

Interventions

     Goal- To encourage students to use their smartphones more sparingly, we should implement more controlled manners of owning smartphones and convince the parents to let us not abuse our online presence since it may be dangerous.

Objectives:
Time regulations for mobile devices during class and break times for a more controlled smartphone usage habit.
An age limit to allow the usage of smartphones especially the younger students and children so they become more aware that using smartphones instead of studying can be detrimental
Insure that smartphone addiction will decrease after following disciplinary measures in turn reducing the likelihood of addiction.

Actions:
Set limits: 
Have a timeframe and use it within that time frame ex. Having a 2 hour time limit for morning, afternoon and evening
Only use your smartphone when the situation calls for it, for example an emergency, and taking down notes.

Create a schedule: 
Use your phone during certain times per day like 2 hours in the morning and 2 hours in the evening so you can focus on the more important things in life.
Be disciplinary on your child’s screen time per day like sitting a limit for a certain time of the day.

Use app blockers: 
Only use these for children below the age of 18 so they don’t accidentally look something they did not intend to look up such as online pornography and website with shady activities
If you insist, put one on your smartphone an so you don’t  accidentally open a specific app that you still need to he installed for work or professional purposes.


Timelines:
     From infancy to the age of 13, children shouldn’t have mobile devices with free access to the internet, it should only be with contacts for family members and friends, for parent approved video games, and educational resources with a time limit for leisurely activities. 
     From ages 13 to 15, some restrictions should be lifted or relieved like extending the timeframe from 30 minutes to 1 to 3 hours and allowing them to use search engines with safe search on with parental controls
     From ages 15 to 18/21, more restrictions should be lifted due to being close to becoming young adults and since some of the topics or future projects can require the safe search engine to he turned off and the timeframe should be close to none since they are now more responsible with the resources they have in their fingertips.
Expected outcomes:
     A decrease of recorded smartphone addicts and an increase of responsible smartphone using students.
     A lot less fights happened due to being rather respectful in the internet resulting with better relationships
     Improved mental health due to less dependence on smartphones for online attention.
     Better academic performance because the usage of smartphones is to help with school such as research and other subjects.
