Chapter 5

Findings, Conclusion & Recommendations

This final chapter consolidates our research insights, presents the overarching conclusion of the study, and lists actionable strategies for students, parents, and educators.

Findings Conclusion Recommendations
Summary

Research Findings

Based on the statistical analysis of the surveys collected from 242 Senior High School respondents, the key findings are:

  • Addiction Prevalence: The majority of respondents fall under the "Neutral" category (55.79%), indicating a moderate, controlled level of daily smartphone use. However, 18.18% are "Moderately Addicted" and 1.24% are "Strongly Addicted," highlighting a vulnerable group of students.
  • Academic Performance: The overall academic GPA for the first quarter was 89.9 (Very Satisfactory), with 64.46% of respondents achieving an "Outstanding" score (90-100). No student fell below the passing threshold of 75.
  • Addiction vs. GPA Correlation: Pearson's r correlation coefficient is 0.0345 (p-value = 0.59329). This demonstrates that there is no statistically significant relationship between smartphone addiction levels and first-quarter academic performance.
  • Triggers & Risk Factors: Key factors contributing to student smartphone dependency include: lack of awareness of screen consequences, personal predisposition, boredom, social pressure, and using phones as coping mechanisms to escape stress or isolation.
Insights

Conclusion

Based on the findings, the study concludes that smartphone addiction does not significantly influence the academic performance of Senior High School students at Dumanjug National High School. Although a notable portion of the student body (37.19% in study habits, 24.79% in physical discomfort) show symptoms of moderate smartphone addiction—such as spending longer periods on screens than intended—their quarterly grades remain outstanding or very satisfactory.

This implies that students may be limiting their screen time during crucial study blocks, or are highly capable of balancing smartphone leisure with academic responsibilities. The extremely weak positive correlation (r = 0.0345) and high p-value (0.59329) demonstrate that screen dependency alone is not a direct predictor of academic failure, and highlights the potential role of other factors—such as personal motivation, family support, and study skills—in securing high academic marks.

Action Plan

Recommendations for Digital Wellness

To prevent mild habits from developing into compulsive addictions, the researchers recommend the following 13 strategies for students:

1

Set screen limits

Establish specific time blocks during the day for phone use, and stick to those boundaries strictly.

2

Create a balanced schedule

Plan your day in advance, allocating dedicated slots for studying, socializing, exercising, and resting phone-free.

3

Use app blockers

Install blocker apps or activate screen time features to restrict access to addictive social media or gaming platforms.

4

Practice mindfulness

Be aware of how screen time affects your mood, sleep, and relationships. Take regular mindful breathing breaks.

5

Find alternative activities

Replace screen scrolling with offline activities you enjoy, such as reading physical books or going for outdoor walks.

6

Cultivate offline hobbies

Engage in hands-on hobbies that keep your hands busy and off your device, like drawing, music, cooking, or sports.

7

Socialize offline

Make a conscious effort to meet friends and family in person, practicing face-to-face communication over texting.

8

Turn off notifications

Disable non-essential app notifications to minimize the temptation to constantly check your phone.

9

Designate phone-free zones

Establish zones in your home (e.g., dining table, bedroom) or times of day (meals, bedtime) where phones are forbidden.

10

Practice digital detox

Designate one tech-free day once a week or every two weeks to fully disconnect and live in the physical world.

11

Use a physical timer

Set a mechanical timer when using your phone for leisure to help you stay alert of how quickly time passes.

12

Seek social support

Talk to family members, friends, or a school counselor about screen addiction struggles, and ask for accountability.

13

Reflect on your triggers

Identify emotional triggers (e.g., boredom, stress, loneliness) that make you reach for your phone, and build healthy coping alternatives.
