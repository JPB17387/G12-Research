Chapter 2

Review of Related Literature

A review of literature addressing the social norms of mobile communication, symptoms of behavioral dependency, and technological, social, and psychological determinants.

Overview

Problematic Smartphone Use

Today’s youth are obtaining access to smartphones at younger and younger ages (Bennet,2020). Although there are many studies in the literature for the factors influencing the use of smartphone, the majority of these studies are for general use of mobile phones. The research on addition of mobile phone is limited (Zuhal and Volkan,2016).

Smartphone addiction, also referred to as problematic smartphone use, is a behavioural addiction characterised by excessive or compulsive use of smartphones. It has become a growing concern, particularly among students, due to it’s potential to interfere with daily activities, including academic performance. This review synthesis key literature on the determinants of smartphone addiction and how it affects academic outcomes.

Smartphone is an electronic gadget mostly used for daily two-way communication (Nair, Vjay & Vinod,2014). It is a device used as a communication tool (Khan, Khalid & labal,2019). In the past few years, it has become so common that individuals, especially youth cannot even think of their lives without using smartphones (Philips & Bianchi,2005)

According to Sheila and Steve (2020) due to the high accessibility and mobility of smartphones, widespread and persuasive smartphone use has become the social norm, exposing users to various health and other risk factors. There is however a debate on whether addiction to smartphone is a valid behavioural addiction that is distinct from similar conditions. However according to Gath et al. (2024) smartphones are an unavoidable part of modern life. While some research suggests that they are incongruous with educational success and well-being for children and youth, other research advocates for the beneficial role it can play at school.

A smartphone is a gadget that can make and receive calls when connected to a network. This gadget does not require a person to stay in one place as they are able to move around within any geographical area that has cell network connectivity. A smartphone gets interconnected to an associated network provided by a mobile phone operator, allowing access to the public telephone network. Today, the smartphone is no longer limited to making and receiving calls. There are value-added services such as text messaging, sending email, online chatting, internet access, photography and gaming. In this borderless global era, mobile communication has become part of our life regardless of region, culture, age and community. Seong and Bo(2018) agreed that smartphone addiction is already entrenched with internet addiction as the symptoms and effects, either positive or negative, are similar. Plant(2001) indicated that there is no standard smartphone effect although the same attitudes, rituals, responses and emotional engagements were discovered. Spending four hours on the smartphone can be considered as addiction, though such criteria of spending seven hours on the smartphone was commonly categorised as a sign of addiction.

Smartphone addiction could be considered a form of technological addiction (Derounian,2017). Nevertheless, apart from the person-to-person description, uncontrollable usage of smartphone can effect academic performance. Smartphone addiction can be categorized as a restlessness symptom associated with attempts to frequently check the phone even though there are no notifications (Ling,2024). Besides that, this addiction is defined as a strong desire to use the smartphone in order to remain occupied by surfing the internet, shopping online and playing games. Students interacting with their phones out of habit can be a way to avoid communicating with parents or teachers. Some students may experience withdrawal symptoms such as insomnia, anxiety, and depression when they are without their smartphone. Rice et al.(2018) exposed that smartphone usage relates to occupation, income and marital status of the potential users. Though many current studies have shown smartphone usage benefits in learning, Woodcock et al. (2012) demonstrated contrasting findings. Students were found always using their phones more for playing games and other leisure activities than for learning. Among the identified factors preventing widespread adoption of smartphone for education are physical, technical and psychological limitations. White and Mills(2012) also found that students were increasingly adopting smartphones with the focus on personal use rather than education. In this study, students were asked if they used smartphone applications in categories in education, dictionary, games and maps. Similarly, Anzai et al.(2013) investigated mobile device use for mobile photo, note-taking, in order to examine the effect of this trend on vocabulary retention.

Determinants of Smartphone Addiction

Psychological Determinants

Several psychological, social, and technological factors contribute to the development of smartphone addiction:

  • Personality Traits:

    Research has shown that certain personality traits are linked with higher risks of smartphone addiction. Chóliz (2012) and Billieux et al. (2015) found that individuals with impulsive tendencies, low self-control, and emotional instability are more likely to engage in compulsive smartphone use. Additionally, Elhai et al. (2017) showed that traits like neuroticism are associated with excessive smartphone use as a coping mechanism for emotional difficulties.

  • Anxiety and Stress:

    Smartphone use as a stress-relief tool has been explored in multiple studies. Elhai et al. (2017) concluded that students with higher levels of anxiety and stress tend to use smartphones excessively, seeking comfort through social media and entertainment apps. Leung (2008) supported this, showing that stress and smartphone overuse form a vicious cycle, where stress leads to overuse, which in turn exacerbates stress.

  • Social Anxiety:

    Hong et al. (2012) demonstrated that socially anxious individuals often turn to smartphones to escape real-life social interactions. The reliance on virtual interactions increases their likelihood of becoming addicted to smartphones as they avoid in-person communication.
